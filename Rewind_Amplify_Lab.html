The problem

Something is hidden inside the signal.

A laser drives a tiny light source. A detector records one bit per time step: click, or no click. Out of sight, a defect can trade energy with the source and quietly change the pattern.

A driven two-level emitter leaks into a measured output mode. A hidden two-level system exchanges one excitation with it. The detector sees a binary record; the memory that shapes it is never measured.

Who has this problem

  • Quantum-chip teams. Unseen defects drain their qubits between calibrations.
  • Photon-source engineers. Two sources can look equally bright and behave differently.
  • Anyone reading a detector. A detector fault can look exactly like a defect.
  • Superconducting qubits. Two-level-system defects set coherence limits and drift in time.
  • Solid-state emitters. Charge and spin environments reduce photon indistinguishability.
  • Inference. Readout dead time or error can imitate a coupling, biasing the fitted model.

Our model

A recurrent machine whose memory is quantum.

Every time step repeats the same four moves. The memory is two qubits that are never looked at. Only one bit comes out. We call it a QRSM: a quantum recurrent state machine.

Each step applies RY(θ) on the emitter, RZ(ζ) on the defect, a Givens exchange(φ), then emission into a light qubit that is measured and reset. The memory evolves under the Kraus operator Ky chosen by the recorded bit y.

Watch a click: the emitter empties, because the photon carried its energy away. The defect keeps what it had. That leftover is the hidden memory.

K1 = sin α |0⟩⟨1| on the emitter: a click projects the emitter to ground and leaves the defect conditioned. No click also updates the state. That back-action is the recurrent update.

The question, and why it is expensive

Given a click at step 3, what happens next?

The useful runs are the rare ones. To study them the ordinary way, you run the machine again and again and throw away every run that missed.

Rejection sampling costs 1/p runs per accepted sample. Here p = P(click at step 3). For one real 64-step record in our first project it was 27,495,891 runs.

Our method

You can’t amplify what you can’t rewind.

Grover’s trick makes rare answers common, but it must run the machine backwards, and a reset cannot be undone. So we keep every output in a fresh qubit. Now the whole run is one reversible film.

Deferred measurement turns T steps into one unitary A with A|0⟩ = Σy |my⟩|y⟩. One iteration is Q = A S0 A† Sf. It reweights the marked histories and leaves their conditional distribution untouched.

What the query returns

One click. Two futures.

With no defect, a click empties the source and the machine starts over. With a hidden defect, something remembers. The pattern after the click tells the two apart.

With k = 0 the click operator resets the memory, so P(click at 3 + τ | click at 3) equals the fresh-run rate at step τ. A coupled defect survives the click and suppresses the later rate: the model’s version of g⁽²⁾(τ).

Follow-up research · photon sources

Equally bright. Not equally good.

Photonic chips need photons that match. Two matching photons that meet at a beam splitter always leave together. A hidden environment that changes between them breaks that, and no single detector notices.

Hong–Ou–Mandel interference: coincidences occur with probability (1 − M)/2 for overlap M. A two-state charge trap shifts the emission frequency. Each one-photon marginal is unchanged; M falls only when the trap switches between the two photons.

Both sources send as many photons, in the same mix of colours. Only the pairs tell them apart. It is the click record again: the memory shows in the pattern, not in the count.

Model: lifetime 1 ns, trap offsets two linewidths apart, 20% single-photon survival, ideal g⁽²⁾(0) = 0. The environment here is classical, so a hidden-Markov model is a fair competitor.

Follow-up research · laser monitoring

Is the laser drifting, or the sensor?

A laser’s colour shifts at a moment nobody knows, and a monitor has 32 seconds to catch it. Adding a quantum sensor, a cloud of 64 atoms, helped a little. A second ordinary reference helped far more.

Unknown-onset frequency-step detection on 128 s synthetic records at 698 nm. The atomic channel is a Ramsey sequence on 64 unentangled atoms with T2 = 200 ms. Alarms are calibrated on held-out sessions; detection counts only within 32 s of the onset.

Follow-up research · chipmaking

Sharper lines, paid for in photons.

Chips are printed with light. In a resist that needs two photons at once, entangled pairs draw fine stripes with about twice the contrast of laser light. More cells print correctly, and it costs more photons.

Crossed 800 nm beams on a two-photon resist: the 400 nm harmonic appears for N = 2 NOON light and for phase-cycled laser light alike, with different contrast. Poisson activation events, a threshold of 20, loss, jitter, blur and a linear background. An assumed resist, not a material on the shelf.

What we claim

  • A recurrent quantum model made reversible, with identical click statistics.
  • Amplitude amplification on its histories, with the conditional answer preserved in the ideal case.
  • A lighter, noise-aware build that beat the textbook build on real IBM hardware.
  • Every number traceable to saved counts, circuits and tests.

What we do not claim

  • No quantum speedup today. At this size an exact classical sampler is faster.
  • No real defect was detected. IBM qubits played the roles of a programmed model.
  • No photons, lasers or wafers were measured.
  • Amplitude amplification is established prior work. Ours is the mapping and the noise-aware design.

Next experiment

Fit candidate models to held-out, calibrated device records and compare against a strong classical hidden-Markov baseline. Then ask where a larger hidden memory makes the classical sampler’s cost, which grew about 6× per hidden qubit in our tests, the bottleneck.
