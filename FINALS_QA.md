# Finals Q&A (V3)

Updated 12 November 2026. Rehearse the twelve questions below aloud, with all four members taking turns. The longer answers after them are for depth. If you do not know an answer on the day, say so and point to the repository: an honest "we did not test that" scores better than a guess.

## Twelve questions to rehearse

**1. What is the problem, in one sentence?**
A hidden defect can change what a detector records, and from the record alone you cannot tell it from a detector fault. Teams building quantum chips and photon sources need models that separate the two.

**2. Why would a quantum computer help?**
Studying a rare event by ordinary sampling costs about 1/p runs. Amplitude amplification costs about 1/√p calls of the model. For one 64-step record that is 27 million runs against about a hundred calls. And the model's memory is quantum, so a quantum circuit is its natural simulator.

**3. So is this faster than a classical computer?**
No. At this size an exact classical sampler answers in a fraction of a millisecond. Its cost grew about 6× per added hidden qubit in our tests, so the question only opens for larger hidden memories. We have not shown a crossover.

**4. What exactly did you build in Qiskit?**
The recurrent model as a circuit. A reversible version with a fresh qubit per step. A one-gate oracle for "a click at this step". The inverse preparation and an ancilla-assisted reflection. Steering. Transpilation to IBM's CZ basis on a fixed layout, with readout-calibration circuits in every job. The package has 114 tests.

**5. What ran on real hardware?**
Nineteen jobs on IBM Kingston and Marrakesh. The featured run is 8,192 shots per circuit on Marrakesh, job `dbqb1ifv5hds73dfo310`. The lighter build hit the click 6,300 times, 76.90%, with 58 two-qubit gates. The textbook build hit it 5,674 times, 69.26%, with 125.

**6. Is that 7.6-point gap real?**
Within the run, yes: the 95% interval is 6.3 to 9.0 points. Across the three runs that day the lighter build led every time, by 7.6 to 14.5 points. The interval covers shot noise only, not drift between runs.

**7. Did fewer gates cause it?**
That is the leading explanation, not a proof. With no noise the two builds tie. A noise model with only gate and readout errors predicts a gap of 7 to 9 points. But the two circuits also differ in width, in routing, and in which qubit reads the click.

**8. A higher hit rate is not a correct answer. How do you know the answer is right?**
We do not assume it. We measure how far the accepted histories are from the exact conditional distribution: 0.118 for the lighter build and 0.181 for the textbook build. Both are biased. On hardware the two futures came out as 8.79% and 11.66%, against the exact 4.67% and 10.10%.

**9. What noise analysis or mitigation did you do?**
Three things. Echo circuits showed the chip was about 1.5 times noisier than its calibration said. A noise-model simulation of the exact compiled circuits predicted 78.4% and 69.7%, against the measured 76.9% and 69.3%. Dynamical decoupling and twirling, on identical circuits, made the answer error worse. The headline numbers are raw counts.

**10. Did you detect a real defect?**
No. We programmed both models and ran them on IBM's qubits. Telling a real defect from a detector fault needs calibrated records from a real device, competing models, and a blind test. That is our next experiment.

**11. What is original here?**
Not amplitude amplification, which is established work. Ours is noticing that a recurrent model with resets cannot be amplified, making it reversible with identical statistics, steering one gate so a single round is enough, using a lighter reflection, and checking the conditional answer instead of only the hit rate.

**12. What did you learn, and what comes next?**
More hits is not more truth. On today's hardware the lighter circuit wins. And one of our own follow-ups went against us: for laser monitoring, a conventional reference beat our quantum add-on, 98% to 58%. Next we want to fit the model to held-out, calibrated device records against a strong classical baseline.

### Five harder ones

**What does your noise model leave out?** Idle decoherence, crosstalk, leakage and drift. One scale factor, 1.54, stands in for all of them. It under-predicts the damage to our deepest circuit, 182 gates, so depolarizing noise is too kind to long circuits.

**Why did plain sampling give the wrong order on hardware?** Because the event is rare. The click should appear in 3.95% of shots and appeared in 7.6%, so about half of the accepted shots were false clicks. Our noise model predicts this. Amplifying the event first lifts it above that floor. It happened again on 12 November, as predicted (slide 41).

**What do the photon, laser and chipmaking studies have to do with your method?** They share one question: after a rare event, was it a one-off, or is something hidden remembering? We asked it of our own machine on IBM (slide 40). A two-photon resist prints on pairs, so the chip version is how often a second photon follows a rare first: exactly 8.5% with the hidden defect and 17.4% without. Rewound, IBM gave 16.8% and 21.0%: the right order, about half the gap. Plain sampling gave 47% and 34%: backwards. For lasers we stepped the hidden defect's frequency and the reading moved from 16.8% to 21.0%. Our qubits played a programmed machine. Nothing here measured a chip, a laser or a photon.

**Did you predict anything before running it?** Yes, once. Before the 12 November job we froze its circuits and wrote down what a noise model said IBM would return. Every direction held, and three of five sizes were inside the measured intervals. It was written twelve minutes before the job, by us, and not published first, so we call it our own record and not a preregistration.

**Is steering cheating?** It is classical importance reweighting, and we say so. It changes one emission angle, which scales every history containing that click by the same factor, so conditional answers are unchanged. It needs a rough value of the click probability.

**Why is the local reflection only approximate?** A full reflection keeps the state in the two-dimensional plane Grover's algorithm rotates in. Reflecting on three of five qubits lets a little amplitude leak out of that plane. In the ideal circuit the cost is 0.035 in total-variation distance.

**How would this scale?** Width grows by one qubit per time step, and depth by one model run per amplification call. The classical exact sampler's cost grows about 8ⁿ in the number of hidden qubits n in theory, about 6× per qubit in our tests. Our hardware runs of this model are small, about a dozen qubits, so scaling is an estimate and not a result.

**What is the problem, and who might use this?** Device researchers want to learn whether hidden memory changes an emitter's detection pattern. We ask the model what happens after a selected rare click. Ordinary rejection sampling discards every run without that click. Our quantum construction returns more matching samples while checking whether their conditional behavior remains accurate. At this small size, direct classical methods are faster. The separate laser-sensing study asks whether another reference helps a source-monitoring engineer identify changes.

**What is a click, in ordinary language?** It is a detection recorded as one in a time bin; zero means no detection recorded. In an optical experiment this can be an on/off photon-detector output. In our IBM experiment a superconducting qubit represents that output of a model. A click is not automatically a defect alarm. A click record is a time sequence of measured outcomes, not a complete observation of the hidden quantum memory.

**What useful answer does the original project return?** It predicts the later click pattern given a selected click. In the original exact example, the probability of a click at step 6 given a click at step 3 is about 4.67% with the modeled hidden defect and 10.10% without it. This difference motivates testing candidate models against data. The query allows all earlier outcomes; it is not restricted to the illustrative prefix `0 0 1`. Full ideal amplification preserves the conditional answer. Local reflection is approximate and requires its own accuracy checks. Reproduction: `research-validation/original_after_click_verification.json`.

**Does that identify the real cause of a detection?** No. It distinguishes predictions of specified models. It does not observe the hidden memory directly or prove that a physical light source has a defect. Several causes can fit the same observations; actual diagnosis needs calibrated measurements, alternative models and independent validation.

**Why do less noisy qubits have value here?** Coherent interference produces the amplified answer. Errors can distort it or reduce useful acceptance, and additional shots cannot remove systematic bias by themselves. Better qubits and fewer error-prone gates can therefore increase the amount of trustworthy information per execution. We still have to measure the actual circuit's accuracy and cost; nominal calibration is not enough.

**What cost reduction did you actually measure?** In the fresh Marrakesh precision repeat, local/full circuits used 58/125 native two-qubit gates and returned 6,300/5,674 accepted shots out of 8,192. That is 75.42/180.47 two-qubit gate executions per accepted shot. This narrow proxy excludes calibration, one-qubit operations, readout and compilation; selected-history errors were 0.1177/0.1814. Different operations, routing and drift remain confounders. It is not a dollar saving, equal-quality answer cost or a speedup over classical computation. The older 40/85-gate pair is separately archived Kingston evidence.

**How could this matter economically for chipmaking?** If a diagnostic reliably identifies source changes, engineers could investigate them before more out-of-spec exposures and avoid some unnecessary stops. That could affect rework and downtime. Our simulation measures none of those financial or production outcomes. A wrong diagnosis can also cause an unnecessary stop, so false-alarm rate, maintenance burden, calibration time and apparatus cost belong in the test.

**What does the QRSM add to defect analysis?** It connects a sequence of observed bits to a physical model of an emitter interacting with hidden memory. Each observed bit selects a measurement update of the modeled quantum state; that state then predicts the next bit. The probabilities of successive bits give the likelihood of the complete record. Across many records, classical fitting can compare candidate coupling, detuning and noise parameters, then test their predictions on unused records. This can help assess whether a proposed hidden interaction explains the data. Earlier fitting used synthetic records; the IBM demonstration uses known model settings. Neither establishes discovery of an unknown physical defect.

**Why does that matter for quantum state measurement?** Hidden interactions can change the actual state before readout, while readout errors can change which bit is reported. If either is omitted, the analysis may attribute the observed statistics to the wrong state or physical cause. Calibrated defect-and-noise models can support more reliable state inference and inform calibration or device choices. One click does not reveal an arbitrary quantum state; general state reconstruction needs repeated preparations and informative measurement settings. Our project does not perform complete state tomography. Physical context: [Schlör et al., 2019](https://www.nist.gov/publications/correlating-decoherence-transmon-qubits-low-frequency-noise-single-fluctuators). Measurement-inference limitations: [Keith et al., 2018](https://www.nist.gov/publications/joint-quantum-state-and-measurement-tomography-incomplete-measurements). These publications motivate the problem; they do not validate our surrogate.

**How does the conditional state update work?** For an ideal model, the observed outcome y selects the operator K_y. Its probability is Tr(K_y ρ K_y†); divide K_y ρ K_y† by that probability to obtain the conditioned state for the next step. This represents measurement backaction and retained hidden memory within the assumed model. It is distinct from estimating coupling parameters across many records. The original code implements these operators in `rewindandamplify-main/qrsm_grover/model.py`; noisy candidate models can instead use outcome-conditioned channels.

**Is this something a classical sequence model cannot do?** Classical hidden Markov models also capture memory. The QRSM contributes physical quantum structure and candidate parameters whose interpretation can be tested. In the fresh joint-inference study, fixed-setting predictive differences from the trained HMM were unresolved. Controlled settings favored the physical likelihood, which shared known parameters across controls while the HMMs were fitted separately. Every likelihood calculation ran classically. The original two candidate models also have different average click rates; we have not shown that timing is necessary to distinguish that pair.

**What concrete evidence shows why detector modeling matters?** In a fresh synthetic cohort, ignoring one-bin detector dead time gave coupling 0.069 when the generator used 0.65. Modeling the blind bin moved the fit to 0.706 and improved held-out likelihood by 0.4584 nats per 24-bin record. The physical state continues evolving while the detector is blind. The corrected fit still misestimated other parameters, including efficiency, and put dephasing on a bound. Correcting one mechanism improves prediction without proving a unique defect diagnosis. Source: `qrsm_photon_poc/followup/README.md`.

**Where does amplification fit into defect detection?** After specifying the model, amplification samples histories satisfying a chosen condition more often. The QRSM supplies the model and conditional predictions; fitting evaluates possible physical explanations. Amplification does not collect more observations from a real source, create extra training information, or make a physical detector more sensitive. Its potential value concerns the cost of asking difficult models conditional questions, and it still needs an end-to-end comparison with strong classical methods.

**What did the earlier defect-fitting experiment teach us?** In an archived synthetic noisy-data example, clean models selected one defect for a zero-defect simulation and two for a one-defect simulation. Adding relaxation and dephasing selected the simulated counts, zero and one. However, the candidate sizes were informed by simulated truth: {0,1} for zero defects and {0,1,2} for one. This was not a blind discovery test. Correct counts also left drive and coupling biases of +6.9 and −3.5 Hessian standard deviations in the noisy one-defect fit. The lesson is to test noise assumptions and parameter recovery, not just the selected count. Source: `rewindandamplify-main/prior_work/hidden_defects/results/who_finds_summary.md` and `rewindandamplify-main/prior_work/hidden_defects/experiments/2_who_finds.py`. This archived pipeline depends on missing legacy v5 code and was not freshly reproduced in this revision.

**Why not use a classical computer?** At the tested sizes it is faster. In this special independent-site toy, the conditional law can be derived and sampled directly without rejecting complete histories. A classical importance sampler also supplies a meaningful baseline. **Can you state that exact conditional sampler?** For n independent sites with absorption probability p, given at most one absorption, the all-zero history has probability (1-p)/((1-p)+n*p), and each one-hot history has probability p/((1-p)+n*p). Sample zero versus one absorption, then choose a uniform site if there is one. This relies on the toy's equal, independent sites and simple threshold.

**What about the more efficient oracle?** For the seven-site toy predicate, a reversible counter used 317 generic CX gates and ten circuit qubits, versus 1,098 CX and seven qubits for enumerating failing histories. This is compiler-specific and trades workspace qubits for gates. A synthetic density-matrix noise experiment is not a real-device result; its layouts differ and do not isolate the oracle's effect.

**Why not add more amplification rounds?** Ideal matching probability changes with the number of rounds and can overshoot. Deeper circuits also accrue noise. In one synthetic seven-site noise model, rounds 0, 1, 2 and 3 gave higher raw matching rates but conditional-history TVDs of about 0.002, 0.072, 0.096 and 0.131. That is one simulated target, with different compiled layouts, not a universal optimum or a hardware result.

## Semiconductor quantum-dot questions

**What did we actually learn, rather than simply adding another application?** We checked three requirements for diagnosis. First, equal photon counts and zero multiphoton emission can hide very different interference quality: our four illustrative cases have overlaps 1.00, 0.50, 0.20 and 0.25. Second, memory changes a paired observation even when each photon's frequency distribution stays fixed: persistent versus independently redrawn offsets give overlap 1.00 versus 0.60. That memory is classical. Third, real published data require a trustworthy observation model: the last corrected contrast changes from 0.7724 to 0.7938 under an alternative normalization. These are lessons about measurement choice, correlations and calibration; they are not new discoveries of the underlying optical laws.

**How exactly does that connect to the original QRSM?** The original QRSM asks how hidden dynamics change the probabilities of later observations. A dot's optical transition could be its emitter, and a candidate local environment could be its hidden state. But the original instrument produces sequential binary outcomes, while HOM jointly measures two photons. We have now implemented and checked that sequential emitter/environment and joint-photon observation model, including real IBM digital emulations. It is not fitted to a physical dot. Passive observations can miss memory, the initial 64-pair test was uninformative, and a separately controlled design needed thousands of attempted pairs. Strong classical HMMs predict equally well; nuisance changes can cause false diagnoses. Quantum computation is not established as necessary for this small model.

**What decision could a semiconductor engineer make with a validated version?** Qualify or tune a photon source for a quantum photonic chip using delivered counts, multiphoton suppression, interference quality and usable-pair yield together. Check optical alignment and references before blaming the emitter, then compare candidate physical models and classical alternatives under controlled bias, excitation or temperature changes. Test predictions on unused records. This is a proposed workflow, not a device qualification, improved fabrication process or manufacturing saving already demonstrated by the team. Existing integrated dot devices provide the physical context: [Davanco et al.](https://www.nist.gov/publications/heterogeneous-integration-chip-quantum-photonic-circuits-single-quantum-dot-devices).

**Where would amplification enter?** After an appropriate physical model and measurement instrument exist, a reversible implementation could query rare conditional histories. That is the original algorithmic question. Amplification does not acquire more real photons or recover coherence information missing from a count record. A useful quantum implementation would still need a full cost-and-accuracy comparison against the strongest classical method.

**How do quantum dots connect quantum physics to semiconductors?** A semiconductor structure confines carriers so that selected transitions have discrete quantum energies. An optically active GaAs or InGaAs dot can emit photons for a quantum photonic circuit. Electrostatic silicon spin dots are another platform; this study models the optical emitter. It does not establish fabrication or operation of either device by our team. The [quantum-dot study](quantum_dot_bridge/README.md) provides platform distinctions and primary sources.

**Why can a bright single-photon source still be unsuitable?** Brightness counts delivered photons. The HBT quantity g⁽²⁾(0) tests multiphoton emission. Hong–Ou–Mandel (HOM) interference tests whether two photons occupy matching optical states: identical photons tend to exit the same port of a balanced beamsplitter, suppressing coincidences between its two outputs. Phase noise, frequency mismatch, polarization or timing errors can reduce that suppression. Photonic interference operations therefore need more than a bright count stream. Our one-photon model imposes the same 20% per-photon survival and g⁽²⁾(0)=0 in all four cases, yet their overlaps are 100%, 50%, 20% and 25%.

**Does a reduced HOM signal diagnose a defect?** It establishes a measurement feature under the stated calibration. Different mechanisms can reduce interference. Our toy model shows how dephasing and differential detuning affect overlap; its two-state offsets have classical pair memory. Neither that model nor three aggregated experimental delay settings identifies a microscopic trap, its switching rate or a coherent hidden qubit. Source, detector and polarization/timing controls are needed for causal attribution. The QRSM can supply candidate conditional predictions once an appropriate measurement model is defined; amplification cannot recover information absent from the acquired data.

**What does the new analysis of real data contribute?** It checks integrated central and reference peaks in six externally published GaAs-dot histograms, declares a normalization protocol, and separates count/calibration uncertainty from changes caused by analysis choices. The authors' exact windows and run normalization are not supplied, so this is a conditional reanalysis rather than an exact reproduction. Its most useful outcome is to show what can be extracted and which calibration choices still limit interpretation. See [the analysis protocol and results](quantum_dot_bridge/hom_analysis/README.md). These are the authors' measurements, not new FGCU laboratory data.

**What is the cost connection?** With independent survival probability eta for each photon, a usable pair survives with probability eta². Reducing eta from 0.8 to 0.2 requires 16 times as many excitation attempts for the same expected surviving-pair count. That calculation excludes background, calibration, correlated loss and apparatus costs; it is not a monetary saving. Filtering may improve conditional photon quality while reducing collection. Report both quality and yield.

**Does this make a lithography-laser sensor?** Semiconductor quantum-light source characterization is a direct device application. A sensor for a lithography laser needs an additional wavelength-compatible coupling scheme, calibrated response, adequate bandwidth and conventional reference comparison. The GaAs HOM data do not validate a DUV/EUV monitor or a wafer-process benefit. Keep that transfer as a separate research question.
