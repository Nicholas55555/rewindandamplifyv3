The real-world problems this work is for

Hidden defects make chips unreliable

Microscopic two-level defects drift in and out of resonance with qubits. They are the leading explanation for the largest swings in qubit lifetime.

This work: learns a defect from one qubit’s clicks, with no probe. Shown in simulation.

Calibration data can miss them

Compilers choose qubits from reported error rates. On ibm_marrakesh, the qubits ours chose showed 1.5% to 3.4% error per gate, against about 0.2% reported.

This work: a short echo circuit caught it, on a real chip.

Error correction has to find them first

Fault-tolerant codes can quarantine a bad qubit, whether it came from fabrication or appears mid-run. But something has to detect it.

This work: a candidate detector from rare click patterns. Not yet tested inside a code.

Studying rare events is expensive

IBM charges $96 a minute on pay-as-you-go. Matching one 64-step record by brute force takes at least 2 hours: about $12,000.

This work: 125 machine calls with steering and Grover, on future hardware.

Beyond computers

Single-photon sources for quantum networks blink as charge traps switch. Labs study this through click correlations, the same kind of signal we read.

Where we stand

Defects found in simulation, and circuits tested on two real chips. We have not yet read a real defect from a real chip’s clicks.

Klimov 2018; Carroll 2021; Siegel 2023; Berhane 2017; IBM pricing, Nov 2026. Cost: 27.5 million shots at our measured 283 µs a shot, a lower bound.

So why does this matter outside a hackathon? Four real problems. First, microscopic defects drift in and out of resonance with qubits, and they’re the leading explanation for the biggest swings in qubit lifetime. Second, calibration data can miss them. In our own runs, the qubits that looked best on one IBM chip were several times worse than reported, and a short echo circuit caught it. Third, error correction can quarantine a bad qubit, but something has to detect it first. Fourth, studying rare events costs real money: at IBM’s rate, matching one record by brute force is about twelve thousand dollars. The same signals appear in single-photon sources too. To be clear about where we stand: we’ve found defects in simulation. We haven’t yet read a real defect from a real chip.
